# Summary of Changes — v1.2-draft

**Draft for Planning Board review, October 14, 2026.** Changes are stated against v1.1-draft, which
is the Core Zoning Code adopted at Town Meeting on September 14, 2026, with layout corrections only.
This draft has not been adopted. Nothing in it takes effect unless Town Meeting votes for it.

This draft carries one amendment: **Right to Farm.** It treats farming as a spectrum — from a
vegetable garden in any neighborhood to full-scale farming in D1 — and leans toward allowing it.
A redline cannot show everything, so the parts a marked-up page hides are explained here: the new
section as a whole, the new symbol in the use tables, the district tables themselves (printed as
they now stand, unmarked), and the renumbering of Article 7.

## Articles in this release

Each of these Articles ships with its own extract and redline: Article 1, General Standards;
Article 2, District Standards; Article 4, Site Standards; Article 5, Building Standards; Article 7,
Use Standards; Article 9, Definitions. Articles 3, 6 and 8 are unchanged.

## What Right to Farm does

### Home farming needs no permit

Growing food, keeping bees, and keeping animals for your own household — and selling from your lot
what you grow or make there, including from a farm stand — needs **no permit** wherever the Code
allows it by right. The Code's general standards, such as setbacks, still apply. Moving from one
tier to another, for example from a garden to a few goats, is not a change of use. Household pets —
dogs, cats, and animals kept indoors — are not farming and are not affected.

**Commercial farming** means producing farm products on the lot to sell elsewhere, wholesale, or for
processing. It keeps its Use Permit from the Code Enforcement Officer, in the same six districts as
today: D1, D2, SD-Conservation, SD-Highway Commercial, SD-Rural Highway and SD-Campus.

### What may be kept where

Home farming comes in five tiers. Each district's use table now shows them, and Article 7 Section 3
sets them out in Table 7.1.

| Tier | What it covers | By right | By Planning Board special permit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cultivation | Growing plants at any scale | Every district | — |
| Beekeeping | Hives, registered with the State | Every district | — |
| Small Stock | Up to six hens, ducks, quail or pigeons (no roosters or drakes) and up to six rabbits | Every district | — |
| Medium Stock | Goats, sheep, alpacas, miniature horses and donkeys; roosters, turkeys, geese, guinea fowl; more than six birds or rabbits | D1, D2, D3, SD-Conservation, SD-Rural Highway, SD-Campus | D4, D5, D6, SD-Historic, SD-Highway Commercial, SD-Marine, SD-Fabrication |
| Large Stock | Cattle, horses, pigs, llamas, emus, donkeys and mules | D1, D2, SD-Conservation, SD-Rural Highway, SD-Campus | D3 |

There is no limit on the number of medium or large animals. Instead, animal housing, and pens, runs
and paddocks, must be set back from property lines (Table 7.2): 10 feet for a coop, 50 feet for
goats' and sheep's housing, 100 feet for cattle and horses' housing. Housing must also be 25, 100
or 150 feet from a neighbor's house, measured to the house itself; a neighbor who builds later does
not put an existing barn out of compliance. Medium and large stock are allowed by right on lots at
least 120 and 220 feet wide; on a narrower lot the Planning Board may approve shorter distances, but
not less than half. Open pasture needs no setback.

### A change for new animals in the village districts

Until now the Code said that private, non-commercial farming was permitted in every district,
subject to the nuisance standards. Under this amendment, **new** goats, sheep, roosters, or more
than six hens in D4, D5, D6, SD-Historic, SD-Highway Commercial, SD-Marine and SD-Fabrication — and
new cattle, horses or pigs in D3 — need a special permit from the Planning Board. Large stock is not
allowed in those village districts at all. **Animals kept lawfully today may stay**, at their
present number and place, without any permit.

### What still goes to the Planning Board

Everything else agricultural is allowed by right. Only these need a Planning Board special permit
(Table 7.3): slaughter offered as a service; processing for wholesale; bringing in more than 50
cubic yards a year of outside organic material; agritourism events with more than 50 people or more
than 12 events a year; large barns and large hoop-house areas in the village districts; manure
storage of more than 50 cubic yards close to a property line, a neighbor's well, a public water
supply or a water body; the reduced setbacks above; and medium or large stock where the table above
calls for a special permit.

### Barns, hoop houses and coops

Each district's buildings table gains an **Agricultural Building** column. Barns up to 5,000 square
feet in D2, 3,000 in D3, and 2,000 in D4, D5, SD-Historic, SD-Highway Commercial, SD-Marine and
SD-Fabrication are permitted by the Code Enforcement Officer; larger ones go to the Planning Board.
D1, SD-Conservation and SD-Rural Highway have no size limit. Hoop houses and high tunnels are not
buildings: they no longer count against the small-structure limit or have to come down after three
months. Coops and hives are allowed in every district, up to 100 square feet where small structures
are otherwise not allowed. Farm fences follow the Code's ordinary fence rules.

### Farming already under way is protected

Any farming lawfully going on when this Section takes effect may continue at its present scale and
location, without a permit, and is not made nonconforming. Nothing has to be altered or removed.

### A trade the Board and voters should see clearly

Maine's Agriculture Protection Act (7 M.R.S. §154) says a farm operation in an area where farming is
permitted cannot be found in violation of a town ordinance if it follows the State's best
management practices. **Because this amendment permits farming in every district, that State
protection may reach farm operations anywhere in town, and may override standards of this Code.**
For example, the Code bans barbed wire; a cattle farm following the State's practices may be able
to use it. How far the protection reaches is set by State law, not by this Code, and is among the
questions with Town counsel. It covers farm operations as State law defines them — not every
backyard garden or six hens. Whether a farm follows those practices is decided by the State's
Commissioner of Agriculture, not by the Code Enforcement Officer, and the farm carries the burden of
showing it.

The Code also adds its own rule: a farming activity that meets Section 3's numbers is not a nuisance
under the Code's nuisance or lighting standards just because of ordinary farm noise, odor, or dust,
except while the Planning Board is deciding a special permit for it.

### Farm exemptions stay with the farm

Several existing exemptions for farms — fence materials, field review, extra outbuildings, outdoor
storage, and screening of work yards — now apply only to the farming activity's own part of the lot,
not to an unrelated business on the same lot, and never to an area used to store or sell products
brought in from elsewhere. The larger ones apply only to commercial production, medium or large
stock, or at least half an acre under cultivation. Farm task lighting used while work is under way
is exempt from the lighting rules.

## The new symbol in the use tables

The use-table legend on every district page gains a fifth symbol, an **open circle (○), meaning "No
Permit Required."** A use marked ○ is allowed without a permit but must meet the standards of Article
7 Section 3. A ○ never appears together with another symbol in the same box.

## The district tables

The district pages are printed as they now stand, without redline marks. Every change to them is
listed item by item in the separate **Use Table Changes** document. In brief: each of the thirteen
districts gains six rows — Farmstand and the five home-farming tiers — and eight districts gain an
Agricultural Building column (D1's existing column is renamed to match). No existing use changed its
status.

## Other changes

- **Definitions (Article 9).** Fifteen terms are added, including Agriculture, Home/Personal
  Agriculture, Commercial Agriculture, Small, Medium and Large Stock, Farmstand, Barn, Hoop House,
  High Tunnel and Best Management Practices, and the two conflicting definitions of "Agricultural
  Building" become one. "Agricultural Use", "Large Animals" and "Small Animals" are removed.
  "Agriculture" now means both home and commercial farming wherever the Code uses it.
- **Horses.** Keeping your own horses is large stock. Boarding other people's horses, lessons,
  shows and events remain a Stables / Riding Center use, as before.
- **Permits (Article 1).** The rule that anything "Permitted" needs a permit now excepts home
  farming under Article 7 Section 3.
- **Renumbering.** The new Article 7 Section 3 moves the sections after it down by one: the former
  Sections 3 through 66 are now Sections 4 through 67. The redline shows this as renumbering, not
  as a change of substance.
- **Corrections.** Fifteen use sections cited the nuisance standards as Article 7 Section 1.G; they
  now cite Section 1.H, where those standards are.

## Not in this amendment

Seasonal housing for farm workers, named in the Comprehensive Plan, is a planned follow-up and is
not part of this draft.

## Before adoption

State law (7 M.R.S. §155) requires the Town to send any proposed ordinance affecting farming to the
Maine Department of Agriculture, Conservation and Forestry at least 90 days before the meeting or
public hearing that considers adopting it. Several legal questions — including how far the State
protection reaches, what counts as a "water body", and whether every citation is exact — are with
Town counsel.
