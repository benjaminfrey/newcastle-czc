# Right to Farm and the Comprehensive Plan

**How the proposed amendment carries out the 2018 Newcastle Comprehensive Plan — for the Planning
Board**

*October 14, 2026. Prepared for the Board's review of v1.2-draft, the Right to Farm amendment to the
Core Zoning Code (new Article 7 Section 3, with related changes to Articles 1, 2, 4, 5 and 9).*

## Why this memo

Maine law expects a town's zoning to be consistent with its comprehensive plan (30-A M.R.S.
§4352(2); Town counsel to confirm the citation). This memo sets out where the June 2018 Newcastle
Comprehensive Plan calls for what the Right to Farm amendment does. Page numbers are the Plan's
printed page numbers. Section references are to the amendment as drafted in v1.2-draft.

## The Plan's call, and how the amendment answers it

### 1. Give farmers room to succeed

The Plan's "Freedom to Farm" discussion concludes that "zoning should give farmers the latitude
needed to be successful if farming is to be a significant part of Newcastle's rural identity"
(p. 67), and the Farm Models discussion adds that "zoning should not stand in the way of new farm
business models" (p. 69).

**The amendment** makes that latitude its stated purpose. Article 7 Section 3.a treats Agriculture
as a spectrum from a garden in any neighborhood to full-scale farming in D1, and directs that where
the Section is silent it "is to be read in favor of allowing Agriculture" (Section 3.a.2).

### 2. Farm stands everywhere

The Plan names farm stands first among the farm business models that "should all be permitted"
(pp. 69–70).

**The amendment** permits a Farmstand in all thirteen districts with no permit at all, shown by the
new ○ "No Permit Required" symbol in every district's use table (Article 2; Article 7 Section 27).
Selling from the lot what is grown or made there is Home/Personal Agriculture (Article 9).

### 3. As-of-right, administered by staff

The Plan recommends that the new code "allow broader activities as-of-right, administered
predominantly by staff using a simple application form" (p. 70).

**The amendment** goes further for home farming: growing food, keeping bees and keeping animals
within the tiers needs no permit (Section 3.c.1). Commercial Agriculture keeps a Use Permit issued
by the Code Enforcement Officer. Only the activities listed in Table 7.3 reach the Planning Board,
and the Code Enforcement Officer may not send other farm projects to the Board (Section 3.c.5).

### 4. Standards that rise with intensity

The Plan describes a matrix in which, "as intensity of use increases, and depending on neighborhood
context, corresponding performance standards would apply" (p. 67), and asks for context-sensitive
standards "to minimize conflict between neighbors and maintain rural character" (p. 70; see also
p. 124).

**The amendment** is built on exactly that principle. Home farming comes in five tiers, from
cultivation to large stock, each allowed where the district can carry it (Table 7.1). Separation
distances grow with the tier, and the lot width needed to keep medium and large stock by right
grows with them (Table 7.2). The most intensive activities — slaughter for sale, wholesale
processing, large events, large barns in the village districts, and large manure storage near
neighbors or water — go to the Planning Board (Table 7.3).

### 5. Agriculture in the rural sector

The Plan's G-1 Rural Sector exists in part for "supporting agriculture, forestry, open space"
(p. 124), and the Plan describes it as including agriculture and forestry lands (p. 119).

**The amendment** gives the rural districts the fullest permission. In D1, SD-Conservation and
SD-Rural Highway every tier is allowed by right, and Agricultural Buildings have no size limit
(Table 7.1; Table 7.3).

### 6. Process on the farm

The Plan says farmers "should be permitted to harvest, process, package on site" (p. 67), and lists
"processing and distribution models" among the farm business models that should be permitted
(pp. 69–70).

**The amendment** defines On-Farm Processing (Article 9) and permits washing, sorting, drying,
packing and processing for sale direct from the farm by right. Processing for distribution off the
farm is also permitted, by Planning Board special permit (Table 7.3).

### 7. Bring the public to the farm

The Plan describes farms "adding programming and activities that invite people to be part of farm
life" — open farm days, slow-food dinners, trails and recreation (p. 67) — and points to
agritourism as a way to transform and diversify the rural economy (p. 102). It defines agritourism to include farm
tours, hay and sleigh rides, crop mazes and pick-your-own (p. 111).

**The amendment** defines Agritourism in the same spirit — tours, pick-your-own harvests, farm
meals, workshops and seasonal festivals (Article 9) — and permits it by right, with Planning Board
review only for events of more than 50 people or more than 12 events a year (Table 7.3).

### 8. Gardens and food growing at home

The Plan invites residents to plant gardens in the Village ("Free-Range Gardening", p. 79) and
anticipates "big gardens and food growing" on larger lots in Academy Hill (p. 139).

**The amendment** permits cultivation at any scale in every district with no permit (Table 7.1;
Section 3.c.1), and permits hoop houses and high tunnels without counting them against the
small-structure limit or requiring their removal after three months (Section 3.g.5).

### 9. Farmsteads and rural building patterns

The Plan proposes the Connected Farm and rural compound building groups as ways to grow while
keeping rural character, and describes traditional farmsteads adding buildings over time
(pp. 118, 168).

**The amendment** keeps the Connected Farm building group intact (Section 3.g.4) and adds an
Agricultural Building column to the buildings table of each district where barns are allowed, so a
farm can build a barn larger than the 30 by 40 foot limit on other accessory buildings (Article 2;
Section 3.g.1).

## What residents said

The Plan records residents' concern about "the desire to keep as much land as possible available
for agricultural production" (p. 20), and their wish to support farmers "so they could make a
living" (p. 131). The amendment's protection for farming already under way (Section 3.i), and its
broad permission for new farming at every scale, respond directly to that concern.

## Conclusion

On each of these points the amendment carries out what the Comprehensive Plan asks of the zoning
code: room for farm businesses, farm stands everywhere, as-of-right permission administered by
staff, standards that rise with intensity, the fullest permission in the rural sector, on-farm
processing and agritourism, food growing at home, and the farmstead building patterns the Plan
proposes.
