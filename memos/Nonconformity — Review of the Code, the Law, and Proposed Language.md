# Nonconformity

**A review of the Core Zoning Code's nonconformity provisions, the Maine law that governs them,
and proposed language — Planning Board discussion memo**

*September 17, 2026. Prepared after the adoption of CZC v1.0 at the Special Town Meeting of
September 14, 2026. This memo reviews what the Code says about nonconformity, checks it against
Maine law and against what other Maine towns do, argues for and against the choices it contains,
and proposes replacement language. It stops short of a draft amendment: nothing here has been
written into the Code.*

*Shoreland zoning is out of scope by direction. It cannot be ignored entirely — the mandatory
shoreland minimums override contrary local text by their own terms — so this memo notes the
boundary where it matters and leaves the shoreland ordinance itself for separate work.*

> **On the citations.** Every statute, rule and case cited here was run to a primary source during
> the research behind this memo. That research also turned up five case names that circulate in
> secondary summaries of Maine nonconformity law and **do not exist**, and two citations that
> automated tools reported incorrectly from the right page. Before any of this text reaches a
> warrant article, a brief or a Board decision, **town counsel should cite-check it on Westlaw or
> Lexis.** Treat the reasoning as sound and the citation numerals as provisional.

---

## 1. The short version

1. **None of this text is ours.** Every nonconformity provision in the Code — the general section
   and all ten per-topic subsections — came word for word from the Code the Town adopted on
   November 3, 2020, and was carried into v1.0 unchanged. The only nonconformity law this project
   wrote is Article 3 §14, Nonconforming Thoroughfares. Both broken cross-references in the area
   are inherited too.
2. **The Code regulates nonconformity in twelve places with no rule about which one governs.** A
   burned building triggers four provisions that give four different answers.
3. **Maine gives almost no help.** Outside the shoreland zone, the state's zoning enabling act does
   not regulate nonconformities at all. Whatever the ordinance does not say, the common law fills
   in — usually against the Town.
4. **Three provisions look legally exposed:** the six-month abandonment rule for uses, the
   unstandardized assignment of a building type to a nonconforming building, and the two
   definitions in Article 9.
5. **One is a live gap the form-based structure created:** the Code makes most existing buildings
   nonconforming as to form, and then offers no variance route for form standards.
6. **Two things are better than peer practice and should be defended:** the burden-of-proof rule in
   §25.d, and Article 3 §14.

## 3. The legal frame

### 3.1 Maine leaves this to us

Maine's zoning enabling act, 30-A M.R.S. ch. 187, contains **two** uses of the word "nonconforming"
in the entire chapter. There is no general statute on nonconforming lots, uses or structures, and
no general merger statute. (30-A §4358, often cited for nonconforming lots, is *Regulation of
manufactured housing*.) Outside the shoreland zone this article is home-rule drafting on a
common-law canvas: **every silence is filled by a default the Town did not choose.**

### 3.2 Two canons that point in opposite directions

> "zoning provisions that restrict nonconformities are liberally construed, and zoning provisions
> that allow nonconformities are strictly construed"
> — *Day v. Town of Phippsburg*, 2015 ME 13

> "A goal of zoning is to eliminate, not perpetuate, nonconformance."
> — *Hill v. Town of Wells*, 2021 ME 38 ¶ 28

Against that sits the ordinary canon that provisions restricting the use of property are construed
strictly against the municipality. The practical consequence is the point: **an ambiguous
nonconformity clause is genuinely unpredictable in Maine.** Precision is not stylistic.

### 3.3 Burden of proof — the sentence to copy

> "It was Seymour's burden to prove the status of the new gravel pit as an existing nonconforming
> use." — *Town of Levant v. Seymour*, 2004 ME 115 ¶ 19

The Levant ordinance itself codified the allocation, and the Court enforced it:

> "The burden of proof establishing that any non-conformity is a legal non-conformity shall, in all
> cases, be upon the owner of such non-conformity and not upon the Town of Levant."

**But the burden flips.** Once nonconforming status is established, the Town bears the burden of
proving a later illegal expansion (*Mason v. Crooker-Mulligan*, 570 A.2d 1217, 1220 (Me. 1990)). A
burden clause that over-claims will be read against the Town on the expansion half.

### 3.4 The drafting source Maine actually has

DEP's shoreland guidelines, 06-096 C.M.R. ch. 1000 §12, are the most tested nonconformity text in
Maine, and most towns copy them. They bind only in the shoreland zone, **but nothing stops the Town
from borrowing their drafting town-wide**, which is the single cheapest way to make our provisions
recognisable to a reviewing court. The pieces worth borrowing are quoted in §6.

### 3.5 What state law compels regardless of what we write

30-A M.R.S. §4364-B: an accessory dwelling unit **must** be allowed on a lot that does not conform
to the zoning ordinance "if the accessory dwelling unit does not further increase the
nonconformity"; one ADU is exempt from density calculations; the Town may not impose an
owner-occupancy requirement. Any nonconformity rule that flatly bars added dwelling units on
nonconforming lots is preempted to that extent.

### 3.6 The relief valve the statute offers, which we do not use

30-A M.R.S. §4353(4) sets a four-part conjunctive undue-hardship test that is close to
unwinnable. But §4353(4-C) ends with this delegation:

> "A zoning ordinance also may explicitly delegate to the municipal reviewing authority the ability
> to approve development proposals that do not meet the dimensional standards otherwise required,
> in order to promote cluster development, to accommodate lots with insufficient frontage or **to
> provide for reduced setbacks for lots or buildings made nonconforming by municipal zoning. As
> long as the development falls within the parameters of such an ordinance, the approval is not
> considered the granting of a variance.**"

Two conditions: it must be **explicit** in the ordinance, and it does not reach the shoreland zone.
This is the statutory hook for handling nonconforming-lot and nonconforming-building relief as a
permit rather than a variance. **We have not taken it.**

## 4. The critique

### F1 — Four answers to "it burned down" · *severity: high*

| Provision | What it says |
|---|---|
| Art 8 §25.f | Zoning Permit within one year of damage or destruction, one-year extension available; otherwise no reestablishment unless it conforms |
| Art 5 §15.c.9 | A destroyed Main Building Mass "must be rebuilt to meet the standards of this Code" — no window, no restoration option |
| Art 6 §5.d.3 | A damaged or destroyed roof "may be repaired or rebuilt to its previous condition" — no conformity requirement |
| Art 7 §1.f.5 | "When a structure containing a nonconforming use is damaged or destroyed, the use may be reestablished" |

One fire triggers all four. The Code has three conflict rules — text beats graphics (Art 1 §2.c.1),
this Code beats other ordinances (§4.c.3), state and federal law beat this Code (§4.c.4) — and
**none for a conflict between two provisions of this Code.** Art 1 §2.d.2 makes the problem worse by
declaring that "All of the terms is this Code are legally binding, whether or not they are numbered.
Purpose and Applicability sections are binding regulations." Every one of the four is binding text.

*Maine check.* There is no inherent right to rebuild; it "hinges on whether the ordinance expressly
allows it… even where the original building was destroyed by fire or natural disaster" (MMA,
Planning Board Manual, citing *Sprague*). And where two clauses collide, the Law Court harmonises
them rather than picking a winner — in *Wolfram v. Town of North Haven*, 2017 ME 114, the expansion
clause won and the casualty clause was held not to reach the case at all.

### F2 — Article 6 contradicts itself · *severity: high*

§1.b.2 exempts renovations and reconstruction of nonconforming structures from the Design Standards
entirely; §1.b.3 grandfathers removed components for 12 months; §2.f.4 requires a destroyed
component to be rebuilt to Code. All three are binding under Art 1 §2.d.2.

### F3 — Both definitions are defective · *severity: high*

**"Nonconformity"** requires that the thing "at the time of its legal establishment, was in
compliance with the zoning regulations." Anything built before Newcastle had zoning was never in
compliance with zoning regulations, because there were none. On its face the definition **excludes
the largest class of nonconformities in town.** The tested formulation is DEP's: allowed "solely
because it was in lawful existence at the time this Ordinance or subsequent amendments took effect."

**"Nonconforming Conditions"** states an operative rule inside a definition, says "this Ordinance"
in a Code that calls itself "this Code", and ends "complies with this section" — there is no section
in Article 9.

*Maine check.* This is the exact failure mode in *Oliver v. City of Rockland*, 1998 ME 88, where a
use-only definition simply did not reach a nonconforming structure in conforming use. MMA frames the
omission of any of the three categories as a constitutional problem, citing *Sprague*.

### F4 — The v1.0 amendment may not be covered · *severity: high, and new*

§25.a.1, §25.b.1 and §25.d.1 key to what existed "prior to the adoption of this Code". The Town
amended the Code on September 14, 2026. Does "this Code" mean the Code adopted in 2020, or as
amended? "Nonconforming Conditions" says "or subsequent amendments"; "Nonconformity" and §25 do not.
Article 3 §14 avoids this by keying to "the effective date of this Article." **Anything made
nonconforming by the v1.0 amendment is currently protected only by the weaker of the two
definitions.**

### F5 — The most consequential decision in the Code has no criteria · *severity: high*

Art 8 §25.e.2 requires the CEO's written interpretation to "assign a building type, components, roof
type, and any other features to the nonconforming building, to set the baseline for modifications
that can be legally made." Art 5 §15.c.3–5 then measures every permitted expansion against "the
assigned building type." Article 9 defines "Assigned" as determining the "closest reasonable"
feature — that is the entire standard. No criteria, no findings, no notice to the owner, no
recording.

Two aggravators. Art 8 §7.c.3 says a written interpretation "cannot contradict or override any
standards of this Code" — yet under §25.e.2 the interpretation *is* the standard for that building.
And research across national form-based codes found **no code that formally reassigns a
nonconforming building to its closest building type.** The mechanism is a Newcastle invention. That
is not a criticism of the idea, which is a sensible answer to a real form-based problem — it is a
reason it needs criteria, because there is no borrowed practice to fall back on and no deference
available for an official's unguided choice.

### F6 — The appeal clock can run before the owner knows · *severity: medium*

Art 8 §23 gives 30 days from the CEO's action. §25.e requires no notice to the owner. The
cross-reference that would have imported the Clerk-filing requirement of §7.d.1.c does not resolve
(F14).

### F7 — Undefined and circular triggers · *severity: high*

"Routine repair and maintenance", "destroyed", "damaged", "requires total replacement", "requires a
total reconstruction" appear throughout and are defined nowhere. "Destroyed and requires total
replacement" is circular. Under Art 1 §2.b.2, an undefined term takes its Webster's meaning — which
is how a $200,000 dispute gets decided by a dictionary.

*Maine check.* This is precisely what cost Rockport its case: with "no more nonconforming" undefined,
the Court applied the restrictive reading (*Lewis v. Town of Rockport*, 1998 ME 144), and DEP then
wrote the definition Rockport had lacked. Note also that Art 3 §14.d.2 defines "substantial
reconstruction" for thoroughfares, and Art 4 §3.j.2 then uses that term for parking lots, where it
is undefined and resolves to the dictionary instead.

### F8 — Two abandonment clocks, and the wrong word · *severity: high*

Article 9 defines "Abandoned" as vacant and unoccupied "for a period of one year or more"; Art 4
§12.e.5 and Art 5 §15.c.12 hang consequences on that. Art 7 §1.f.4 makes six months of discontinued
"active and continuous operation" an abandonment of a nonconforming use.

*Maine check.* The word matters more than the period:

> "To prove abandonment there must be 'some clear and unmistakable act or series of acts' to
> indicate a purpose to relinquish a right… The party asserting abandonment bears the burden of
> proving both the intention to abandon and actual abandonment… Non-use, even for lengthy periods
> of time, is, of itself, insufficient to show an abandonment of a right."
> — *Pleasant View Mobile Home Park v. Town of Mechanic Falls*, 538 A.2d 273 (Me. 1988)

Saying "abandonment" hands the landowner three things at once: an intent element, the burden on the
Town, and the rule that non-use alone never suffices. Ordinances written in cessation terms
("discontinued", "ceases") have been applied in Maine with no intent analysis at all. The Law Court
has never squarely held that intent is not required — the one appeal that raised it cleanly was
decided on other grounds — so this rests on consistent application plus practitioner guidance rather
than a square holding.

*Peer check.* Across nine Maine towns read in full, discontinuance periods run from 90 days to 36
months and cluster at 12–24. **The only other six-month clause found is a legacy waterfront
provision in Rockland that sits inconsistently beside that town's own two-year rule.** DEP uses one
year, with a good-cause extension and a residential carve-out. Damariscotta — our neighbour — is the
instructive outlier: it treats discontinuance as a rebuttable presumption the Board can excuse for
extenuating circumstances.

§1.f.4 also collides with §1.f.5: if a fire puts a use out of operation for eight months, one clause
says the use is gone and the other says it may be reestablished.

### F9 — No relief route for form standards · *severity: high*

Art 8 §19.b.1 bars use variances by statute. §19.b.2 allows practical-difficulty variances only for
"lot area, lot coverage, frontage and setback requirements." Massing, building type, roof, shopfront,
component and frontage-composition standards have **no variance route**. In a use-based code that
gap is narrow. In a form-based code it is the main event: the form standards are what most existing
buildings fail. The only flexibility is the unstandardized assignment in F5, and §4353(4-C)'s
delegation (§3.6) is unused.

### F10 — Dangling categories · *severity: low, but cheap to fix*

Both definitions and §25.a–b govern nonconforming **signs** and **site characteristics**. The Code
has no sign standards and does not define site characteristics. §25.b.1 also lists "roads", which
Article 3 §14 governs on different terms.

### F11 — Building versus structure · *severity: medium*

Article 9: "Building"… "Not synonymous with Structure." §25 governs "structures"; Art 5 §15 governs
"buildings" (reaching "buildings, accessory buildings, and additional structures"); Art 6 §1.b.2
says "structures or components"; Art 1 §1.b.5 says "use, structure, or activity." *Oliver* is the
case that punishes this.

### F12 — Nonconforming lots are two sentences · *severity: high*

Art 2 §2.d says nonconforming lots may not be further subdivided, and that a lot nonconforming as to
width or area may be developed if setbacks are met. There is no merger rule, no treatment of adding
land to or dividing a nonconforming lot, and no reference to the State Minimum Lot Size Law.
Article 9 makes "Lot" synonymous with "Lot of Record", collapsing a distinction other codes rely on.

*Maine check.* Merger must be **express** — Maine courts will not imply it (*Moody v. Town of Wells*,
490 A.2d 1196 (Me. 1985); *Powers v. Town of Shapleigh*, 606 A.2d 1048 (Me. 1992)) — and merger
clauses are read narrowly (corner lots, landlocked back lots, lots fronting different streets). The
governing statute is 12 M.R.S. §§4807-A to 4807-D, under which contiguous lots in the same ownership
on or after October 3, 1973 are considered one lot, subject to the §4807-D exemption. Where a local
minimum differs from the State's, the ordinance controls the merger question — so silence here is a
choice, and not one the Town has made deliberately.

### F13 — Art 1 §1.b.5 overshoots · *severity: medium*

Read literally it applies the whole Code to any "alteration, enlargement, or discontinued use of a
nonconforming use, structure, or activity." Every detailed section contradicts it.

### F14 — Stale cross-references · *severity: low, inherited*

- Art 8 §25.e.1 cites "Article 8.A.6 Written Interpretation"; written interpretations are Art 8 §7.
- The district pages (Article 2) cite "Article 5 Section 3 Nonconforming Buildings" twice;
  nonconforming buildings are Art 5 §15, and §3 is Building Assembly.

### F15 — Drafting drift · *severity: cosmetic*

"non-conforming" hyphenated five times in Articles 2, 5 and 6; "this Ordinance" three times; §25.f
lettered a–c where every sibling uses numbers; "All of the terms **is** this Code" in Art 1 §2.d.2.

## 5. What to keep, and defend

**§25.d, the burden of proof.** Across nine peer ordinances, burden of proof is essentially
unaddressed as a nonconformity-specific issue. Ours states it. That is ahead of local practice and
aligned with *Levant*. It needs one qualification (the *Mason* flip) and a workable evidence
standard, not replacement.

**The permissive posture of the per-element rules.** Routine maintenance allowed, alteration toward
conformity allowed, repair of partial damage allowed. National form-based practice says exactly this:
treat structures more permissively than uses, and expect form nonconformity to be the normal case
rather than the exception. The per-element sections are more generous than a conventional code, and
that is right for a form-based code in a town of pre-1900 buildings.

**Article 3 §14.** Purpose, applicability, continuation, a defined reconstruction trigger, an
explicit rule that a way serving three or four dwellings may continue indefinitely, a reclassification
escape valve, and an acceptance rule. It is the template.

**The "Assigned" concept.** It answers a real problem no other code has solved. Keep the idea; give
it criteria.

## 6. The central choice: consolidate, or keep the per-element structure?

### The case for consolidating into one article

- One place to look; one set of defined terms; no tiebreaker needed because there is nothing to tie.
- It is what most reviewed codes do — Beaufort County, Buffalo, Miami 21, and the Maine peers that
  are best drafted (Freeport's four-part section is the clearest example found).
- It makes the four categories and their cross-products explicit, which is what *Oliver* and MMA's
  constitutional framing call for.
- Conflicts like F1 and F2 cannot survive consolidation; they are artifacts of separation.

### The case for keeping rules per element

- The per-element rules are calibrated to the element. A roof, a shopfront and a parking lot do not
  fail in the same way, and a single rule would either over-restrict roofs or under-restrict lots.
- Users read the Code by subject. An owner replacing windows finds the windows rule in the windows
  article.
- Billings' form-based code does distribute renovation triggers per element, which shows the approach
  is workable — but it keeps them as lettered subsections of **one** section, not scattered across
  seven articles.
- Consolidation is a larger amendment, and a larger amendment is a larger target at Town Meeting.

### Recommendation

**Do both, in the shape Billings uses.** Move everything *general* into Article 8 §25 — categories,
definitions, effective date, continuation, burden, determinations, restoration, discontinuance,
relief — and leave in each article only what is genuinely element-specific, rewritten as an
allowance operating within the general rules. Then add the precedence rule, so that any collision
that survives has a stated answer. This keeps the readability argument, removes the conflicts, and
confines the amendment to §25 plus short replacement subsections.

## 7. Proposed language

*Drafting notes, not adopted text. Text marked **[borrowed]** is quoted or closely adapted from the
source named, which is deliberate: recognisable language is easier to defend. Everything else is
drafted for this memo and needs counsel's eye.*

### 7.1 A precedence rule — new, Art 8 §25.c

> **Precedence.** Where more than one provision of this Code applies to the same nonconforming
> condition, the provision governing that specific element applies, and this Section supplies any
> matter on which that provision is silent. Where two provisions cannot be reconciled, the more
> restrictive governs. No provision of this Code requires the removal, alteration or
> discontinuance of a lawfully existing condition except as expressly provided in this Section.

The last sentence is the important one. **[borrowed]** in structure from Tulsa Zoning Code §1.080's
three-part most-restrictive clause.

*Argument against:* "more restrictive governs" is a landowner-unfriendly default in a code whose
per-element rules are deliberately generous. *Answer:* it only operates after specific-over-general
has failed, and the savings sentence caps its reach. The Board could instead choose "the more
permissive governs for conditions lawfully existing on the effective date" — a real policy choice
worth putting to the Board explicitly.

### 7.2 Four categories, one umbrella — replacing both Article 9 definitions

> **Nonconforming lot** — a lot of record that, on the date it became nonconforming, did not meet
> the area, frontage, or width requirements of the district in which it is located. **[borrowed,
> DEP Ch. 1000 §17]**
>
> **Nonconforming structure** — a structure that does not meet one or more dimensional or form
> standards of this Code, including setback, height, lot coverage, footprint, building type,
> massing, or component standards, but which is allowed **solely because it was in lawful existence**
> on the date it became nonconforming. **[borrowed, DEP Ch. 1000 §17, extended to form standards]**
>
> **Nonconforming use** — a use of a building, structure, premises or land that is not allowed in
> the district in which it is situated, but which is allowed to remain **solely because it was in
> lawful existence** on the date it became nonconforming. **[borrowed, DEP Ch. 1000 §17]**
>
> **Nonconforming condition** — any nonconforming lot, structure or use, and any site feature,
> component, roof, window, door, shopfront, parking area, fence, wall, lighting fixture or
> thoroughfare that does not conform to this Code and was in lawful existence on the date it became
> nonconforming.
>
> **Date it became nonconforming** — the effective date of this Code, or of the amendment to this
> Code, that first caused the lot, structure, use or condition not to conform.

That last definition is the fix for F4, and it survives every future amendment without further
edits. Delete "Nonconformity" and "Nonconforming Conditions". Either add sign standards or strike
signs from the list; the same for "site characteristics".

### 7.3 Increase in nonconformity — new definition

> **Increase in nonconformity** — any change that causes further deviation from the standard
> creating the nonconformity. A change that meets the standard, or that causes no further increase
> in the **linear extent** of the nonconformity, does not increase it. A structure may be expanded
> laterally provided the expansion extends no further into the area of nonconformity than the
> closest portion of the existing structure. Included in this allowance are expansions that in-fill
> irregularly shaped structures. **[borrowed, DEP Ch. 1000 §17]**

This is the definition DEP wrote *after* Rockport lost *Lewis* for not having one. Adopting it is a
deliberate choice of the more permissive rule; without it, the restrictive *Lewis* reading is the
default.

### 7.4 Restoration — replacing §25.f and the four competing rules

> **Restoration.** A nonconforming structure or condition that is **removed, or damaged or
> destroyed, regardless of the cause** **[borrowed, DEP Ch. 1000 §12(C)(4)]** may be restored,
> reconstructed or replaced as follows.
>
> a. Where the loss is **50% or less of the market value** of the structure before the loss,
>    excluding normal repair and maintenance, it may be reconstructed in place to its previous
>    condition if a Zoning Permit is obtained from the Code Enforcement Officer within **one year**.
> b. Where the loss is **more than 50% of the market value**, it may be reconstructed or replaced if
>    a permit is obtained from the Planning Board within **eighteen months**, provided the
>    reconstruction does not increase the nonconformity.
> c. The Code Enforcement Officer may extend either period once, for up to one year, for good cause.
> d. **Voluntary removal.** A structure removed by the deliberate act of the owner may be replaced
>    under paragraph (b), provided a permit is obtained before removal.
>
> **Market value** — the estimated price a property will bring in the open market and under
> prevailing market conditions in a sale between a willing seller and a willing buyer, both
> conversant with the property and with prevailing general price levels. **[borrowed, DEP Ch. 1000 §17]**

Paragraph (d) exists because of *Wolfram*: a casualty clause keyed to damage and destruction does
**not** reach a voluntary teardown, which is the more common case. Market value, not assessed value:
no Maine source endorses assessed value, and the state model defines market value.

*Argument against a percentage test:* it invites appraisal fights, and Miami 21 dropped its
50%-of-value rule for single-family homes after finding it ambiguous about whether the percentage
applied to the structure or the use. *Answer:* name the denominator explicitly, as above. *Alternative
worth the Board's consideration:* drop the threshold entirely for buildings and allow rebuilding to
the prior footprint and dimensions within a fixed window, which is what several codes now do and what
insurers and lenders reward (§8).

### 7.5 Discontinuance — replacing Art 7 §1.f.4

> **Discontinuance.** Where a nonconforming use is **discontinued for any reason for twelve
> consecutive months**, it may not be resumed, and any subsequent use must conform. The Planning
> Board may, **for good cause shown by the applicant, grant up to a one-year extension** of that
> period. This provision does not apply to the resumption of a residential use of a residential
> structure provided the structure has been used or maintained for residential purposes during the
> preceding five years. **[borrowed, DEP Ch. 1000 §12(D)(2)]**

Four changes from what we have: the word "discontinued" rather than "abandonment"; twelve months
rather than six; a good-cause valve; a residential carve-out that protects seasonal camps. Article 9's
"Abandoned" definition stays for the separate purposes of Art 4 §12.e.5 and Art 5 §15.c.12, but should
be renamed **"Vacant building"** so one word does not carry two jobs.

*Argument for keeping six months:* it clears derelict uses faster, and the Town has lived with it
since 2020 without incident. *Answer:* it is unsupported anywhere in Maine, it is out of step with
every peer, and the first time it is enforced against a seasonal or fire-interrupted business it will
be litigated on the word "abandonment", which the Town would have to prove with intent.

### 7.6 The assignment determination — replacing §25.e

> **Determinations.** The Code Enforcement Officer determines whether a nonconforming condition
> exists and, for a nonconforming building, assigns the building type, components, roof type and
> other features that set the baseline for permitted modification. The determination must:
>
> a. state the district, the assigned features, and the standards that will apply;
> b. state the facts and evidence relied on;
> c. explain why each assigned feature is the closest reasonable fit, by reference to the building's
>    existing massing, height, frontage placement, roof form and street relationship;
> d. be issued in writing within 30 days, filed with the Town Clerk, and **delivered to the owner by
>    mail**;
> e. remain in effect for the building unless the building is altered in a way that changes the
>    facts relied on; and
> f. be recorded in a register of nonconforming determinations maintained by the Code Enforcement
>    Officer.
>
> **The appeal period under Article 8 Section 23 begins on the date the determination is delivered
> to the owner.** **[borrowed in concept, Va. Code §15.2-2311]**

Paragraph (c) is the heart of it: it converts "closest reasonable" from an unguided choice into a
reasoned one, which is also what earns the CEO deference on appeal. Paragraph (f) is the registry
question — no Maine peer maintains one, so this is a cost decision for the Board, but it is the
natural home for the scope-of-right record that *Mason v. Crooker-Mulligan* shows is the real
battleground years later.

### 7.7 Relief from form standards — new, and the most substantive proposal

> **Approval of modified dimensional and form standards.** Pursuant to 30-A M.R.S. §4353(4-C), the
> reviewing authority may approve a development proposal for a lot or building made nonconforming
> by this Code that does not meet the dimensional or form standards otherwise required, where the
> proposal meets the standards to the greatest practical extent and does not increase the
> nonconformity. An approval under this Subsection is **not** the granting of a variance. This
> Subsection does not apply within the shoreland zone.

This is the F9 fix, and it uses the statute's own words so that the "not a variance" characterisation
holds. It needs the Board to decide who the reviewing authority is for each case, and it needs
counsel to confirm the shoreland carve-out language.

*Companion option, worth debating:* a **deemed-conforming** clause providing that a building lawfully
existing on the date it became nonconforming is conforming as to form standards for all purposes
except expansion. That is how some form-based codes avoid classifying most of their town as
nonconforming. *Argument against:* it gives up the leverage to bring buildings toward the standards
over time, and Maine's stated policy is that zoning aims to eliminate nonconformities, not preserve
them. *Argument for:* the leverage is theoretical in a town where the standards were written to
describe the existing village, and the cost of nonconforming status is real and immediate (§8).

### 7.8 Burden and evidence — amending §25.d

> The burden of proof establishing that any nonconformity is a legal nonconformity is, in all cases,
> upon the owner of the nonconformity and not upon the Town. **[borrowed, Town of Levant ordinance,
> enforced in *Town of Levant v. Seymour*]** Where the Town asserts that a lawfully established
> nonconforming condition has since been unlawfully expanded or altered, the burden of proving the
> expansion or alteration is upon the Town.
>
> A nonconformity may be established by any credible evidence, including assessor records, aerial
> photography, tax or utility records, dated photographs, recorded instruments, prior permits, and
> sworn statements of persons with personal knowledge.

The second paragraph matters because most Newcastle nonconformities predate permitting entirely;
the current §25.d.2 invites an applicant to produce a prior permit that can never have existed.

### 7.9 Lots — replacing Art 2 §2.d

At minimum the Board must decide, and the text must say, four things: whether contiguous
nonconforming lots in common ownership merge (and if so, which combinations); whether land may be
added to a nonconforming lot; whether a nonconforming lot may be divided if every resulting lot is
"as conforming as possible"; and how all of this sits with 12 M.R.S. §§4807-A to 4807-D. Merger will
not be implied — if the Town wants it, it must say so; if it does not want it, silence produces
uncertainty rather than the answer.

Also required, quoted rather than paraphrased:

> An accessory dwelling unit must be allowed on a lot that does not conform to the municipal zoning
> ordinance if the accessory dwelling unit does not further increase the nonconformity.
> **[borrowed, 30-A M.R.S. §4364-B]**

### 7.10 Housekeeping

Fix the two stale cross-references (F14); normalise "nonconforming"; replace "this Ordinance"; renumber
§25.f; rewrite Art 1 §1.b.5 to defer to §25; define "substantial reconstruction" where Art 4 §3.j.2
uses it, or point it at Art 3 §14.d.2; resolve "building" versus "structure" throughout; and check
whether nonconforming uses are inside or outside the Code's defined term "Permitted", since Maine
holds that a nonconforming use is not a permitted use unless the ordinance says so.

## 8. Why the rebuild rules matter more than they look

An over-strict restoration rule does damage before any fire happens.

- Standard homeowner policies **exclude** the added cost of rebuilding to current code; that cost
  falls on the owner unless they carry an Ordinance or Law rider, typically capped at 10–25% of
  dwelling coverage.
- Fannie Mae's Selling Guide will not purchase loans where land-use rules "prevent the reconstruction
  or maintenance of the property improvements if they are damaged or destroyed."
- FHA requires every appraisal of a nonconforming property to state whether it may legally be rebuilt
  if destroyed. That question is asked at **every** sale and refinance, not only after a loss.
- Title insurers route nonconforming properties to a different endorsement.

So the practical effect of §25.f plus Art 5 §15.c.9, read together at their strictest, is to make
some older Newcastle houses harder to insure, finance and sell today.

**The counter-argument, stated fairly.** Nonconformity is a tolerated harm, not a right. Strict
rebuild triggers are the main lever a town has to bring old siting into line with current standards,
and they keep faith with owners who built to the current rules at their own cost. Maine's own courts
say the policy is elimination, not perpetuation. The Board should choose knowingly between the two,
rather than inheriting the answer from 2020 by default.

## 9. What the Board is being asked

1. **Structure** — consolidate the general rules into §25 and keep element-specific allowances, as
   §6 recommends? Or leave the structure alone and add only the precedence rule?
2. **Discontinuance** — twelve months with a valve and a residential carve-out, replacing six months?
3. **Restoration** — percentage-of-market-value tiers, or rebuild-to-prior-footprint within a window?
4. **Form-standard relief** — adopt the §4353(4-C) delegation? And with or without a deemed-conforming
   clause?
5. **Lots** — merge, or not, and say so.
6. **Registry** — worth the administrative cost?
7. **Precedence default** — more restrictive, or more permissive for lawfully existing conditions?
